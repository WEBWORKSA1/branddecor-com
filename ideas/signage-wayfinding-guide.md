Wayfinding is the system of signs, graphics and cues that helps people find their way through a space. Good wayfinding feels invisible — people simply get where they're going. Poor wayfinding creates frustrated visitors, late meetings and staff who spend their day giving directions. And because every sign carries your typeface, colours and tone, wayfinding is also branding.

## The four sign families

1. **Identification** — names places: building signs, suite numbers, room names, department signs.
2. **Directional** — arrows and destinations at decision points: lobbies, corridor junctions, lift lobbies.
3. **Informational** — directories, maps, opening hours, Wi-Fi details, rules of the space.
4. **Regulatory and safety** — exits, fire equipment, accessibility, no-entry. These often have specific legal requirements.

## How to plan a wayfinding system

1. **Walk the journey.** Start where visitors arrive — car park, street, transit — and walk to every key destination. Note every point where you hesitated.
2. **Map decision points.** Every hesitation point needs a directional sign. Places people pass straight through don't.
3. **Name things consistently.** Use the same names on directories, door signs, website and calendar invites.
4. **Set a hierarchy.** Large directional signs at key junctions, smaller confirmation signs along the way, identification at the destination.
5. **Test with strangers.** Give someone unfamiliar a destination and watch where they slow down.

## Letter height and contrast

A widely used rule is about **1 inch of capital-letter height per 10 feet** of viewing distance for comfortable reading, with around 1 inch per 30 feet as the outer limit of legibility. Contrast matters as much as size: light text on a dark background (or the reverse) with a strong difference in brightness reads far better than brand colours that look similar in value. Our [letter height calculator](/tools.html#t-letter) and [palette contrast checker](/tools.html#t-palette) help with both.

## Accessibility

Accessibility standards (for example the ADA Standards in the United States, and comparable codes elsewhere) set requirements for certain permanent room-identification signs, such as raised characters, braille, non-glare finishes, character proportions, contrast and mounting height. Requirements differ by country and sign type, so involve a sign maker familiar with your local code. Beyond the legal minimum, inclusive wayfinding uses clear pictograms, plain language and consistent placement.

## Materials

| Material | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Acrylic | Room IDs, directories | Clean, affordable, easy to update with inserts |
| Aluminium / ACM | Directional panels, exterior | Durable, weather-resistant |
| Vinyl | Floor and wall directional graphics | Cheapest way to add arrows and zones |
| Digital screens | Directories, events | Easy updates; needs content management |
| Photoluminescent | Exit and safety | Glows in power outages where required |

## Branding your wayfinding

Use your brand typeface (or a highly legible companion), your colour palette for zones or floors, and your tone of voice in informational signs. A playful brand can say "Coffee this way →"; a law firm probably shouldn't. Consistency across every sign is what makes a wayfinding system feel premium.
